Original visualization

Title: Create your Better Life Index

Source: OECD Well-being Data Monitor

Link: oecd.org/en/data/tools/well-being-data-monitor/better-life-index.html

The chart compares well-being across the 38 OECD member countries. Each country is a flower. Vertical position is that country's index after the reader sets a 0-10 weight on each of eleven dimensions: housing, income and wealth, work and job quality, social connections, knowledge and skills, environmental quality, civic engagement, health, subjective well-being, safety, and work-life balance. Petal colour and length show the score on one dimension. Sliders on the left change the weights, and a menu sorts the countries.

The numbers come from the OECD Well-being Database: national survey and official statistics, one or more indicators per dimension, updated on different years. The public tool turns those indicators into a single weighted index and a flower glyph per country.

Analysis of the original

Intended message

Well-being is not one number. A country can rank high on health and low on housing, and the country that looks "best" changes if the reader cares more about income than about safety. The display is built so that change in weights is visible in the ranking.

Audience

The page sits on a public OECD data monitor. It is aimed at a general audience, journalists, and students, not at people who already work with the Well-being Database.

Tasks

A reader should be able to set personal weights, rank countries and find the top and bottom of the list, compare several countries on one dimension, and see where a single country is strong or weak across the eleven parts.

Redesigned visualization

Each column is one country. Names sit on a shared line at the top. Dot height is the weighted score from the sliders; the dot's colour is that country's strongest dimension. The strip of eleven marks under the dots is the full profile, using the same colours as the sliders, with length for the 0-1 score. Click a dimension name to select it; the bar at the bottom of every column then shows that dimension. Sort by overall performance or by the selected indicator. Hover a dot for all eleven scores, or a strip or bar for a single value.

Data source and references

The redesign reads data/bli-headline.csv, built by scripts/prepare_data.py from the OECD Well-being Database SDMX CSV. It keeps one headline indicator per dimension, the latest total-population value, and the 29 countries that report all eleven. Lower-is-better indicators are reversed before min-max scaling to 0-1.

Visualization critique and redesign report

Original visualization and context

The OECD's "Create your Better Life Index" (Figure 1) is a public tool on the Well-being Data Monitor. It ranks the 38 OECD members on eleven headline dimensions of current well-being. Each country is a flower: height is the index under the reader's 0-10 weights, and each petal is that country's score on one dimension. A menu sorts countries by the weighted score.

The intended message is that well-being has several parts, and which country looks best depends on what the reader values. The audience is the general public. Viewers should be able to set those weights, rank countries and find the extremes, compare countries on one dimension, and see where one country is strong or weak.

Critique

The ranking is the strongest piece of the original. Countries are sorted, each flower sits on a vertical drop line, and the rising curve shows Mexico and Colombia at the bottom and Norway, Switzerland, and Iceland at the top. Position on a shared scale is doing that work.

A second strength is the colour match between sliders and petals. Each control uses the same hue as its dimension, and dragging a slider moves the flowers. The weighting task has visible feedback without a separate legend.

Comparison on one dimension is the main failure. Petals leave centres at different heights and different angles, with no shared baseline. To compare safety, a reader has to hunt for the grey petal in 38 flowers and judge unaligned lengths. People estimate aligned length and position much more accurately (Cleveland and McGill 1984). That comparison is one of the tasks the tool asks for, so it belongs on a shared axis.

The glyph is doing too much. One flower marks the overall score and also carries eleven petals. Where scores bunch, as on the right of Figure 1, flowers overlap and hide both height and petals. Names hang under each flower, so they follow the curve instead of a common line. The vertical axis has no title. A small mark for the score, and names on one baseline, would be easier to read.

Eleven hues at once is more colour than people can keep apart. Several pairs are close, such as the two greens and the reds for social connections and work-life balance. Once every petal is coloured, hue turns into texture. The dimension the reader is asking about is the one that should carry colour.

Redesign rationale

I replaced each flower with a small dot on a 0-10 axis. Height is the weighted score. Position is a better channel for magnitude than a large glyph, and dots do not cover their neighbours, which was the overlap problem. Names sit on one line at the top of the chart, so they no longer ride the ranking curve.

For one dimension I draw a bar under every country from a shared baseline, in the same column as the dot. Clicking a name on the left selects that dimension and paints the bars in its slider colour. Two extra sort options order the columns by that indicator. Asking which country is safest is then a row of aligned bars.

The OECD colours remain, but they no longer sit on overlapping petals. The dot takes the colour of that country's strongest dimension. Under the dots, a strip of eleven short bars shows the full profile, with length for the 0-1 score. Hovering a dot opens a card with all eleven values; hovering a strip or bar gives the raw indicator. Colour is used on small aligned marks instead of a halo, and the profile the flower tried to show is still there.

Original versus redesign

It is easier to compare every country on one dimension, to sort by that dimension, and to read the overall order where scores are close. The strip and the hover card also show a country's strengths without decoding petals. The strip is small in a 29-country layout, and the selected bar still shows only one indicator at a time. The numbers are not the OECD's published index. Each dimension uses one headline indicator from the Well-being Database, scaled 0-1 across the 29 countries with complete data. Lower-is-better items such as housing-cost overburden are reversed, and long hours in paid work stands in for work-life balance. Colombia, Turkiye, Israel, Costa Rica, the United States, Chile, Japan, Korea, and New Zealand drop out. Years differ across indicators, and because the dots are averages they can still bunch.
